## Section 1: Introduction to Data Collection

In order to conduct quality research, the nurse researcher must clearly identify and accurately measure the data that is necessary to answer the research question. A data collection plan should be designed to be clear, unbiased, reliable, and valid.

This lesson provides an overview of data collection. Specifically, the types of data, the data collection process, and different data collection methods will be discussed.

---

## Section 3: Data Collection

The selection of a data collection method is dependent on the specific information needed to answer the research question, the characteristics of the concepts of interest, and the resources available to the researcher. Quantitative data is numerical or quantifiable and is analyzed using statistical analysis. Data collected in qualitative studies is descriptive and is often analyzed by categorizing and coding data. Harwood and Hutchinson (2009) recommend the following data collection process: define the purpose, select an approach, select a delivery method, write a protocol, design the forms, educate staff and participants, and transfer the data.

### Image Transcript: Data Collection Process

1. **Purpose** — The goals of the study and research question should be clear and align with the data collection method.
2. **Feasibility** — Consider the resources available, the characteristics of the target population, and the research team skills to determine if there is a feasible data collection approach.
3. **Delivery Method** — Select a specific delivery method that aligns with the study goals and research question (e.g., qualitative studies likely will include focus groups or interviews).
4. **Protocol** — Write a systematic, clear, and detailed plan for all steps of the data collection process.
5. **Design Forms** — Create data collection forms (e.g., surveys or entry logs) that are self-explanatory and efficient for data collectors and/or participants to complete.
6. **Educate** — Train data collectors.
7. **Transfer Data** — Transfer raw data to data analytic software. Monitor for data entry errors.

### Reference

Harwood, E., & Hutchinson, E. (2009). Data collection methods series: Part 5: Training for data collection. *Journal of Wound, Ostomy, and Continence Nursing*, *36*(5), 476-481.

## Section 5: Primary and Secondary Data

Data used to answer a research question may be primary or secondary data. Primary data is original data and secondary data is the analysis and interpretation of primary data collected for another purpose.

**Primary Data**

Primary data is data collected by the researcher (or member of the research team) directly from the subject for the specific purpose of the research study.

- Benefits: Considered the most reliable type of data because it is collected directly from the subject.
- Limitations: Time consuming to collect; the quality of the data depends on the subject (e.g., recall, communication barriers, misrepresentation of sensitive data, reporting what they think the data collector wants) and the data collectors (e.g., word choices, tone, consistency).

**Secondary Data**

Secondary data is information retrieved from data sets that were originally collected for other purposes.

- Benefits: Data is already collected; the sample may be larger than what is feasible to collect as primary data.
- Limitations: Data may be incomplete, inaccurate, or not in a format that is as useful to answer the research question.

## Section 7: Primary Data Collection

Primary data collection can occur in a variety of ways, including physiological measurements, surveys, social media, technology, or by use of a data collector.

### Tab: Physiologic Measurements

Physiological data can be collected by wearable devices or manual methods, such as collecting blood and analyzing it, manually measuring blood pressure, or using a scale to measure weight.

- Benefits: Objective data
- Limitations: Calibration of equipment can affect data

### Tab: Surveys

The most common data collection method is the survey, which is used to collect information directly from participants about their experiences, behaviors, attitudes, or perceptions. The use of paper surveys is a traditional method that requires manual data entry into spreadsheets or software for data analysis procedures. More up-to-date survey methods include online or electronic data collection (e.g., Survey Monkey or Qualtrics) which are efficient, effective, more convenient for research participants, and minimize data entry errors.

- Benefits:
  - Cost effective
  - Can be anonymous
  - Easy to administer to large groups
  - Familiar process for participants
  - Flexible and convenient for participants
- Limitations:
  - Impersonal
  - Low return rates
  - Literacy barriers
  - Time consuming for participants
  - May be unable to prevent multiple submissions

### Tab: Social Media

Data collection or participant recruitment may occur through social media or virtual networks. This data collection method is controversial because individuals access the platforms for social purposes and not all groups of potential participants may have access to social media applications.

- Benefits: Expands the geographical scope of a study to gain access to hard-to-reach populations
- Limitations:
  - Selection bias
  - May be limited by the social media's terms of use or privacy policies

### Tab: Technology

Technology such as tablet devices, mobile apps, audience response systems (e.g., clickers), and Photovoice (e.g., taking pictures and writing an accompanying reflection to share one's lived experience) may be used to collect data.

- Benefits: Improved response rates to sensitive questions
- Limitations:
  - Limits data types to surveys or questionnaires
  - Does not allow for follow-up questions

### Tab: Data Collector

Data collection by a data collector may include interviews, focus groups, and observation. Interviews may be conducted by phone, video conferencing software, or in person. Interviews can be highly or loosely structured.

- Benefits: Content of data can be rich and meaningful
- Limitations:
  - Quality of data is dependent upon the skill of the interviewer, focus group leader, or observer
  - Interviews, focus groups, and observation can be time-consuming and resource intensive
  - Observation may result in the participants not behaving the way that they normally would because they are aware they are being observed
  - Results may not be able to be generalized

## Section 10: Secondary Data Collection

Secondary data is retrieved from data sets that have been collected for other reasons. Examples of data sources include electronic medical records, public health databases, outcome measurement databases, and government sources of health data (e.g., census data).

- Benefits:
  - Can improve the quality of subject recruitment
  - Can reduce the time needed to collect data
- Limitations:
  - Can be costly to access data
  - May be large amounts of data that is difficult to sort
  - May contain irrelevant data for what is being measured

### Image Transcript: Secondary Data Sources

- Patient satisfaction surveys
- Staff and physician surveys
- Report cards from accrediting agencies
- Benchmarking against other organizations
- Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) scores
- Inter-organization performance improvement systems
- Functional efficiencies within departments
