## Section 1: Introduction to Measurement

The credibility of a research study is almost completely dependent on identifying and measuring the right outcome. A strong measurement strategy is essential for good evidence. If the measurement is not correct or is inconsistent, the researcher may draw the wrong conclusions.

A research study may have the perfect design that is well controlled and flawlessly executed, but if the data collected is not consistent and accurate, the results will be unreliable.

Through completing this lesson, you will be able to:

- Identify levels of measurement in data collection instruments.
- Discuss the implications of levels of measurement for statistical analysis.

## Section 3: Measurement

Measurement is most often about assigning numbers or some other classification to subjects in a way that represents the quantity of the attribute numerically. This is typical in medical research. However, measurement is not always so straightforward.

### Tab: Forms

Most forms of measurement involve quantifying a trait or sorting subjects into categories based on characteristics. For example, using a scale to measure body weight or asking about religious affiliation seeks to classify subjects.

Classification is common in nursing research. Classification may occur by a neutral observer, or subjects may classify themselves by completing an instrument that asks for self-rating.

### Tab: Rules

Whether data is collected by instrumentation or classification, rules are used to guide measurement. For example, rules may specify the way a measurement is administered, the timing of the procedure, the exact protocol for collecting the result, how the questions are worded, or how the interviewer should read a question.

### Tab: Numbers

No matter how data is collected, it will likely be represented by a number. Numbers are objective, standardized, precise, and consistent. Numbers can accurately represent attributes, and statistical tests can be applied to numbers to quantity results.

To provide value, numbers must be clearly linked to the research question, appropriate to represent the variable of interest, and consistently accurate.

## Section 4: Determine and Define Research Variables

In the measurement strategy, the first step is to determine and define which variables (attributes) represent the research question. The research question will describe the phenomenon of interest, which must be translated into attributes that can be measured. Attributes may be:

- physical (blood pressure)
- perception (pain)
- behavior (gait)
- response (recall)

After the attributes are identified, they must be defined by the researcher so that misinterpretation is avoided. There are two types of definitions for attribute variables.

### Tab: Conceptual Definition

A conceptual definition describes the variable using other concepts. Conceptual definitions ensure the researcher is measuring the correct thing.

Example: Depression is described as sadness, lack of pleasure, or changes in eating/sleeping habits.

### Tab: Operational Definition

An operational definition describes the operations that must be performed to accurately represent the variable. Operational definitions ensure the researcher is measuring attributes reliably.

Example: Depression is represented by using a scale to measure weight gain or loss, or using a log to record the number of hours of sleep per night.

## Section 5: Determine the Measurement

Next, the researcher defines the procedure (measurement) for collecting data. The procedure differs depending on the type of data to obtain: primary or secondary data.

### Tab: Primary Data

Primary data are recorded by the researcher directly from a subject and are considered the most reliable. Measures for primary data collection include:

- calibrated instruments, such as a thermometer or sphygmometer
- equipment, such as a digital camera
- paper-and-pencil or online questionnaires, surveys, or rating scales
- observing, rating, and reporting behavior or characteristics, such as skin assessment
- counting the frequency of an attribute or action, such as the number of falls on a unit

Primary data collection has some limitations, including that it is time-consuming to collect and the quality of the data depends on many factors related to subjects and data collectors. For example, some subjects may not communicate clearly, not be able to accurately recall, or may misrepresent data. Although self-reporting by subjects is prone to biases, there is no acceptable alternative way to measure many nursing variables of interest, such as satisfaction, pain, depression, and quality of life.

Data collectors may also influence primary data collection by incorrectly using equipment or instruments. They may also use an inconsistent approach or language with subjects. For example, the question "Do you abuse alcohol?" will elicit a far different response than "How many alcoholic drinks do you consume in a week?"

### Tab: Secondary Data

Secondary data is data that has been previously collected by another researcher and will be analyzed for the current research study. Typically, this data comes from large governmental institutions or healthcare facilities. Using secondary data is economical and less time-consuming, but the data may not be specific to the researcher's needs (Benedictine University, 2021).

## Section 6: Collecting Primary Data

The measurement methods most commonly used to collect primary data include physiologic measurements, psychometric instrumentation, surveys, and questionnaires.

### Tab: Physiologic Measurement

Physiologic measurement involves the assignment of a number or value to a subject's biological functioning. Measures may be self-reported (pain), observed (gait), directly measured (weight), indirectly measured (fluid balance), electronically monitored (heart rhythm), or obtained through diagnostic tests (blood glucose).

Physiologic measurement requires the use of specialized equipment and specific training on the use of that equipment. For these uses in a research study, the data must be collected in exactly the same way from every subject. Consistency is very important, and the goal is to have minimal variability in the data collection procedure.

### Tab: Psychometric Instrumentation

Instruments used to collect subjective data from subjects are called psychometric instrumentation. Examples of subjective data include stress, coping, self-esteem, and motivation. It is essential that the instrument chosen for measuring the variable fits closely with the conceptual definition of the variable.

Though some researchers will develop their own instruments, using an existing instrument has many advantages. First, using an existing instrument saves the researcher time as the validation and pilot testing of the instrument has already been done. Developing a new instrument for data collection should only be done when a literature review reveals that no acceptable instrument already exists. Second, using an existing instrument allows for replication of the study and its inclusion in aggregate studies, such as systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

The limitations of using a previously developed instrument include the cost of use, failure to address the research needs, feasibility of administering the instrument, and inability to locate psychometric information or the original author for permission to use it.

### Tab: Surveys and Questionnaires

Surveys are the most commonly used data collection tool, which gathers information from subjects about experiences, behaviors, attitudes, or perceptions. Depending on the types of questions, surveys may provide quantitative, qualitative data, or both. Surveys may be distributed in person, via mail, or online. Responses are described in numeric terms and/or in words.

Questions on a survey can be open-ended or closed-ended. Open-ended questions are typical in qualitative research. Responses are analyzed using content analysis to find themes in the words of subjects. For example, an open-ended question: How did the nurse midwife help you deal with the birth of your baby?

Closed-ended questions are commonly called questionnaires. There are a fixed number of responses for the subject to select as an answer. Closed-ended questions are found in quantitative research and are easier to analyze.

## Section 7: Questionnaires

Closed-ended questions can be forced-choice, dichotomous, or scales. View the slideshow below to learn each type of question.

### Slide 1: Forced-Choice Questions

Forced-Choice Questions require the subject to select a single response from a list of possible answers.

- Sample question: What is your current marital status? (Select one)
- Answer choices: single, married, divorced, separated, and widowed

### Slide 2: Dichotomous Questions

Dichotomous Questions require the subject to select a single response from only two choices.

- Sample questions: Were you born in the United States? (Select one)
- Answer choices: yes and no

### Slide 3: Scales

Scales ask subjects to rank-order their responses on a continuum. The most commonly used scale is the Likert scale that asks the subject to indicate agreement or disagreement with each statement on a five or seven-point scale. Each degree of agreement is assigned a numerical value, so a total can be calculated from all responses.

- Sample statement: The physical layout of my patient care unit is efficient. (Select one)
- Answer options: strongly agree, disagree, neither agree or disagree, agree, and strongly agree.

## Section 8: Measurement Error

Measurement error is the difference between the measured value of the attribute (observed value) and its true value. For example, the average newborn weighs 8 pounds with a margin of error of 2 pounds (+/- 2 pounds). Another example is the difference between what a test score indicates and the actual degree of a student's knowledge mastered.

Some degree of measurement error is present in every instrument. There are two types of measurement error: random error and systematic error.

### Tab: Random Error

Random error is expected and is typically due to human error, confusion, bias, and normal environmental variations. These sources of error are not preventable but should be minimized when possible.

Example: When a subject weighs themselves on a scale daily, they may assume a slightly different position on the scale each time, resulting in a random error.

Example: Measuring height is affected by minor postural changes.

### Tab: Systematic Error

Systematic error has a more serious effect on the study results and is any error that is predictable and consistently biased. The measurement is consistent but not accurate. Typical causes are observational errors, inaccurate instrument calibration, and environmental interference.

Example: An improperly calibrated thermometer can give accurate readings within a certain temperature range but will become inaccurate at higher or lower temperatures.

Example: Measuring a newborn's length will vary when using a new cloth measuring tape versus an older, stretched one.

It is important to find instruments that measure attributes consistently and with minimal unexplained variability. An instrument is not reliable when there is a discrepancy between the true attribute and its representation by the result.

## Section 9: Measurement Reliability

Instruments are considered reliable when they consistently measure an attribute with precision. When a measurement consistently generates an accurate value every time and is used appropriately, it is considered precise. Reliability provides the reader of a research study with confidence that the differences between two studied groups are not explained by the differences in the way the attribute was measured.

Reliability must be internally stable—stable when used among individuals, stable when used among raters, and stable over time.

### Tab: Internal Reliability

Internal reliability means that the way responses vary on one item should demonstrate the same pattern as the way responses vary on the entire instrument. It is measured with Cronbach's alpha and should have a value of 0.7 or greater.

A high level of internal reliability indicates that changes from item to item represent real changes in the subject, rather than changes in the way questions are interpreted. When the measurement error is low, internal validity is high.

### Tab: Stability Among Individuals

Stability among individuals is measured by an item-total correlation, which should have a positive sign and an absolute value close to 0.5. Item-total correlation indicates whether performance on a single item is consistent with the individual's overall performance on all items.

### Tab: Stability Between Raters

Stability between raters is called inter-rater reliability and quantifies the stability of a measure across raters. For example, when two or more nurses stage a pressure ulcer, the results should be similar. Cohen's kappa is used to document inter-rater reliability and generates a p value, reflecting the statistical significance of the agreement between raters.

A Cohen's kappa value:

- Less than 0.4 is considered poor.
- 0.41 to 0.75 is acceptable.
- A value greater than 0.75 is excellent.

For example, a high kappa (greater than 0.75) with a low p value (less than 0.05) reflects good reliability between multiple raters in an experiment. Ongoing training and monitoring of raters can improve agreement and reliability between raters.

### Tab: Stability Over Time

Stability over time is quantified by a test-retest correlation coefficient. The standard reliability coefficient is 0.7 or greater. Test-retest correlation is measured by administering an instrument, waiting a reasonable period of time, then readministering the same instrument. A correlation coefficient can then be calculated between the two sets of item scores.

**NOTE:**

The goal of measurement reliability is to assess consistency with the instrument and among individuals over time.

### Reliability Statistics

| Test | Meaning | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cronbach's alpha | Measures internal reliability. Are the individual items consistent with overall test results? | Should exceed 0.7 >0.9 is strong reliability |
| Test-retest reliability | Measures stability over time. If the instrument is used repeatedly, are the results due to actual changes in the subject? | Should equal or exceed 0.5 |
| Criterion reliability | Measures stability between individuals. Does the instrument measure the actual presence of the attribute it is intended to measure? | Should exceed 0.5 |
| Inter-rater reliability | Measures stability between raters. Do two or more raters agree on the ratings? | Cohen's kappa should exceed 0.75 with a p value less than 0.05 |

## Section 10: Measurement Validity

- Validity vs. Reliability
- Types of Validity

### Tab: Validity vs. Reliability

Instruments are considered valid when they measure the concept, or construct, they are supposed to measure. Reliability tells the reader that the instrument is consistent; validity tells the reader that the instrument will consistently measure the right thing. Reliability does not ensure validity.

For example, if your scale is off by 5 pounds, it will read your weight every day with an excess of 5 pounds. This is reliability because it reports the same weight every day. However, the weight is not valid, because the scale adds 5 pounds to each reading. In this case, the instrument is reliable but not valid.

Validity is more challenging than reliability. Consider another example.

A researcher believes that an individual's self-esteem is reflected by the measurement of the index finger length. This measurement would have excellent test-retest reliability but no validity. Just because one person's index finger length is longer than another's does not indicate increased self-esteem. Again, it's reliable but not valid.

### Tab: Types of Validity

A researcher can use several methods to document measurement validity. One or all types may be used in the same study. View the types of validity below.

#### Tab: Content Validity

Content validity is a subjective judgement about whether a measurement makes sense. Typically, a panel of experts has verified that the correct concepts are included in the measure. The matching between questions and the content the questions are supposed to measure is called content validity.

Example: The biology exam (measurement) should cover all topics (construct) actually taught to students, but not unrelated materials like English or math.

#### Tab: Construct Validity

Construct validity demonstrates that the test is actually measuring the construct it claims to measure. The most common method of construct validation is factor analysis, which groups items within the instrument according to their shared variability. The researcher can then review the factor groups and determine whether they represent the conceptual basis of the instrument.

Example: A researcher wants to know if a specific educational program increases emotional maturity in elementary students. Construct validity would determine if the research is actually measuring emotional maturity.

#### Tab: Criterion-Related Validity

Criterion-related validity is the correlation of the instrument to some external manifestation of the characteristics. It measures how well one measure predicts the outcome of another measure. There are three types of criterion validity.

- Concurrent validity is present when an instrument reflects actual performance. Example: The reading from an oral thermometer correlates with the reading from a temporal thermometer.
- Predictive validity indicates that a measure can predict future performance. Example: A job applicant takes a performance test during the interview process. If this test accurately predicts how well the employee will perform on the job, the test is said to have criterion validity, more specifically, predictive validity.
- Discriminant validity demonstrates the capacity to differentiate those who have a characteristic from those who do not. Example: An instrument to measure the presence of a disease would have discriminate validity if it can accurately diagnose a disease when present and definitely confirm when the same disease is absent.

#### Tab: Sensitivity and Specificity

Sensitivity and specificity are measures of validity used in biomedical research because they measure characteristics of diagnostic tools used to detect disease.

- Sensitivity is the ability of an instrument to detect a disease if it is present.
- Specificity is the ability of an instrument to determine when the disease is not present.

Sensitivity avoids false negatives, and specificity avoids false positives. Both are needed for confidence in study results.

#### Tab: Responsiveness / Longitudinal Validity

Responsiveness is the ability of a measure to detect change overtime in the construct of interest. It is also called longitudinal validity.

Example: When a measurement is intended to capture the effect of an intervention, responsiveness is the capability to detect changes in the subject over time.
