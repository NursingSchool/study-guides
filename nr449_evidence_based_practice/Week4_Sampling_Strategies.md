## Section 1: Introduction to Sampling Strategies

In research, the sample refers to the subset of the population that the researcher intends to study. The sampling strategy describes how the sample was selected from the population. Without a good sampling strategy, study findings may not be credible or generalizable.

At the end of this learning module, you will be able to:

- Explain why sampling is important.
- Contrast probability sampling with nonprobability sampling.
- Identify sampling strategies to estimate the necessary sample size for a research study and avoid selection bias.

---

## Section 3: Target Population and Sample

Subject samples are designed to represent a designated population in a research study. Samples are more efficient and economical to study. They allow the researcher to study a topic of interest without having to reach the entire affected population. Let's review a few important terms below.

**Population**

This is the entire set of subjects who are of interest to the researcher; sometimes called the target population. It is simply not possible to study the entire population of interest.

**Sample**

This is a subset of the target population that the researcher will actually study. If strategically selected, the sample can effectively represent the broader population.

---

## Section 4: Sampling Error

Of course, sampling has a downside. Data collected from a sample is not as accurate as if collected from the entire population, so the study results will never match the population perfectly.

However, researchers use statistics to account for this difference. The statistical measurement is called the sampling error. It is often called "chance" or "standard error" and is used to determine if the study results reflect real effects or occur by chance.

**NOTE:**

The sampling strategy is used to minimize sampling error by ensuring that the sample is closely representative of the target population.

---

## Section 5: Sampling Plan

Whether research is quantitative or qualitative, the sampling plan is important. However, it serves different purposes.

### Tab: Subjects

The sampling plan aims to maximize generalization of the study findings.

The participants in a quantitative study are called "subjects" and are chosen using methods to ensure the sample represents the target population as closely as possible.

**NOTE:**

The main goal of quantitative research is generalizability.

### Tab: Informants

The sampling plan helps to establish credibility.

Instead of subjects, the participants in a qualitative study are called "informants" or "respondents" and are chosen for their ability to provide insight into the research question. Informants are selected from the potential pool of subjects in a controlled manner called purposeful selection. The researcher selects those subjects that are most likely to inform the research question.

**NOTE:**

The goal of qualitative research is credibility rather than generalizability.

## Section 6: Sampling Strategy

The most important parts of the sampling strategy include:

- Subject Number
- Subject Selection
- Subject Assignment

### Tab: Subject Number

An adequate number of subjects is essential to a research study. This provides the study with the ability to accurately detect effects on the outcome of interest, often called the study's power. Power increases confidence that the results of the study are authentic.

Though an adequate sample size is essential, failure to obtain enough subjects may not be the fault of the researcher. Even when the best efforts are spent on designing an effective sampling strategy, conditions beyond the researcher's control may limit the ability to gain enough subjects to power the study.

#### Example

Ritter et al. (2020) aimed to determine the clinical effectiveness of chlorhexidine gluconate compared to povidone-iodine in the reduction of surgical site infections six months after the procedure. A power analysis revealed that 330 subjects were needed to power the study.

Initially, 300 subjects were enrolled in the study. However, only 279 after post-procedure completed the follow-up six months later. Therefore, 51 of the original subjects were lost to attrition, resulting in an underpowered study.

The researchers reported a lack of statistical significance in the results, meaning no differences were found in the number of surgical site infections when using chlorhexidine compared to povidone-iodine (Ritter et al., 2020).

Cook and Hatala (2015) suggested that readers should lack confidence in a study that is underpowered and reports no statistical differences between intervention groups.

A strong sampling strategy requires that the researcher ensures the sample is representative of the target population and the size of the sample is adequate. These steps are critical to establishing that the results can be confidently applied to nursing practice.

**Important!**

Power is controlled by ensuring an adequate number of subjects represent the target population. Attrition occurs when subjects leave a study. The researcher may not know whether they continued or discontinued the intervention; therefore, no data can be considered from these subjects.

### Tab: Subject Selection

The first step of a sampling strategy is to define the population of interest. Terms used to describe a population often include:

- age (adults, children, neonates)
- gender
- diagnosis
- setting
- geographic location

The available population is called the sampling frame, which includes the potential subjects that match the description of the needed population and are accessible to the researcher.

Once the population is defined, a selection strategy is developed to choose the actual subjects from the sampling frame. The goal of the selection strategy is to prevent selection bias.

**NOTE:** Not all terms on the list to the left need to be used to describe a population. Some studies need a more generalized population (see the first example below), while others require high specificity (see the second example below).

#### Tab: Example

Example: In adult surgical clients in the operating room, does the pre-incision use of CHG compared to the use of povidone-iodine impact surgical site infections in 30 days (time)?

- "adult"—indicating age
- "surgical"—indicating diagnosis
- "the operating room"—indicating setting

Example: In post-menopausal women, does hormone replacement therapy compared to no hormone replacement therapy impact the incidence of breast cancer?

- "post-menopausal"—indicating diagnosis, also implying age
- "women"—indicating gender

### Tab: Subject Assignment

The assignment of subjects to groups is impacted by two factors:

#### Tab: Selection Bias

Selection bias means that the sample is not an accurate representation of the population. This problem may occur for two main reasons:

- The researcher influences the selection of subjects.
- The researcher influences the assignment of subjects to groups.

For example, a researcher may want to assess the effectiveness of an influenza vaccine. However, the intervention group primarily includes young healthy adults, while the vaccine is intended for clients who are elderly with comorbidities.

Selection bias is most likely to occur when the researcher has preconceived ideas about what the study results will show or when subjects are selected for a study or assigned to groups in a discriminating way.

A good sampling strategy is the best way to control selection bias. When randomization is used to assign subjects to a study group, selection bias is reduced greatly.

#### Tab: Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when the sample under-represents or over-represents some characteristics. Homogeneity occurs when subjects have very similar characteristics. Both issues make generalizing the study findings to other populations difficult.

For example, a researcher may want to study the effects of working nights on the incidence of health problems and collects data from day shift workers and workers performing the same duties at night. However, the day shift is primarily females who are middle-aged and the night shift is younger male subjects.

Sampling bias arises when the subjects studied are very similar or the two groups compared are very different from the beginning of the study.

### References

Cook, D. A., & Hatala, R. (2015). Got power? A systematic review of sample size adequacy in health professions education research. *Advances in Health Sciences Education*, *20*(1), 73-83. https://doi.or/10.1007/s10459-014-9509-5

Ritter, B., Herlyn, P. K., Mittlmeier, T., & Herlyn, A. (2020). Preoperative skin antisepsis using chlorhexidine may reduce surgical wound infections in lower limb trauma surgery when compared to povidone-iodine: A prospective randomized trial. *American Journal of Infection Control*, *48*(2), 167-172. https://doi.org/10.1016/j_ajic.2019.08.008

## Section 8: Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The use of objective selection criteria and sound recruitment methods are appropriate for all types of research.

- In qualitative research, the sampling strategy is less complicated but purposeful and thoughtful.
- In quantitative research, subjects are recruited based on carefully considered criteria to represent the target population. All subjects who meet the criteria are generally invited to participate in the study.

In most studies, this information is clearly described and can be found in the "Methods" section of the article. Objective selection criteria help minimize bias.

### Tab: Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria provide guidelines for choosing subjects who match a predetermined set of characteristics, which may include clinical, demographic, geographic, and temporal criteria. These criteria help the researcher objectively identify who can be considered a subject.

**NOTE:**

The main function of inclusion criteria is to limit the potential for selection bias.

### Tab: Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria are characteristics meant to exclude a potential subject from the study. For example, some subjects are not appropriate for the study, even though they meet inclusion criteria.

For example, a subject might meet gender, age, and geographic inclusion criteria but have a comorbid condition that could affect study results.

**NOTE:**

Exclusion criteria serve the same purpose as inclusion criteria and help to control extraneous variables.

## Section 12: Sampling in Qualitative Research: Methods for Purposeful Selection

The researcher has an obligation to design the best sampling strategy that will meet the goals of the research study. View the slideshow to learn several methods for purposeful selection.

### Slide 1: Typical Case Sampling

Typical Case Sampling is used when the research study requires informants who have a common characteristic.

- Example: nursing students

### Slide 2: Homogeneous Sampling

Homogeneous Sampling is used when the researcher has a concern that an outlier case may skew responses.

- Example: females of the Muslim faith

### Slide 3: Criterion Sampling

Criterion Sampling is used when the research requires informants that have an experience in common.

- Example: survivors of breast cancer

### Slide 4: Maximum Variation Sampling

Maximum Variation Sampling is used when the research requires informants with diverse characteristics.

- Example: first time mothers and experienced mothers who are breastfeeding

### Slide 5: Extreme Case Sampling

Extreme Case Sampling is used to find informants that have extreme target population characteristics.

- Example: mothers who have experienced the death of a child but who vary in race, socioeconomic status, and number of gestations

### Slide 6: Theoretical Sampling

Theoretical Sampling is used when additional sources of data are needed during grounded theory development.

- Example: studying nurses during medication administration by interviewing focus groups, observing medication delivery, and analyzing data trends for errors

## Section 14: Types of Sampling in Quantitative Research

The goal of the sampling strategy in a quantitative study is an excellent representation of the target population.

### Tab: Probability or Random Sampling

Probability or random sampling is a technique in which every member of the available population has an equal opportunity to be selected as a representative sample. Random selection uses mathematical probability to ensure that the selection of subjects is completely objective.

Independence is ensured when the selection of one subject has no effect on the selection of other subjects. Each member of the population has exactly the same chance of being in the sample.

### Tab: Nonprobability Sampling

Nonprobability sampling occurs when randomization is not used for selecting participants. Members of the available population do not have an equal chance of being selected as subjects.

Example: If the population of interest is nursing students, only students from Chamberlain University are invited to participate in the study.

Sometimes randomization is not possible and the researcher has no choice but to ask accessible subjects to participate in a study. In this situation, bias can be controlled by randomly assigning the subjects to experimental groups. Then any differences between the sample and the population will be evenly spread throughout the groups.

**NOTE:**

Quantitative research has the best generalizability when subjects are selected and assigned to groups randomly. The use of probability in sample selection or group assignment helps the researcher achieve this goal. Randomness and independence are essential.

---

## Section 15: Sampling in Quantitative Research: Probability Sampling

Below are types of random sampling that meet the probability criteria.

### Tab: Simple Random Sampling

Simple random sampling is when a table of random numbers is used to select subjects from the sampling frame (the entire population eligible to participate in the study). The numbers are often computer-generated. The subjects are each numbered and random numbers are drawn for participation.

Example: If numbers 10, 22, and 33 are drawn, then the 10th, 22nd, and 33rd subjects on the list would be asked to participate in the study.

### Tab: Systematic Random Sampling

Systematic random sampling is used when the researcher is unsure how many individuals will eventually be in the population or when there is an indefinite sampling frame.

Example: A researcher may give a survey to every third parent that brings a child into the pediatrician's office.

### Tab: Stratified Random Sampling

Stratified random sampling is structured so that important characteristics are evenly distributed across all groups.

Example: The subjects may be divided into subgroups by age, like 18-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, and over 60.

### Tab: Cluster Random Sampling

Cluster random sampling occurs when the researcher randomly selects entire groups and then randomly selects subjects from only those groups.

Example: The researcher might list all nursing schools in the state and assign each a number. Then random numbers are generated and nursing schools assigned those numbers are selected. Faculty in those schools are chosen to participate in the study.

## Section 16: Sampling in Quantitative Research: Nonprobability Sampling

When random sampling is not realistic, nonprobability sampling methods must be used.

### Tab: Convenience Sampling

Convenience sampling, the most common sample type, occurs when the researcher uses subjects who are accessible to the researcher. This type of sampling is more logical and less costly than probability sampling; however, bias may be introduced into the sample.

Example: If the researcher wants to study nursing students, the researcher can select the sample from the local community college.

### Tab: Snowball Sampling

Snowball sampling is a type of convenience sampling that violates both randomness and independence. The researcher recruits the initial subjects, who are then asked to identify or recruit other subjects into the study. Although this may be the only way to access subjects with certain characteristics (e.g., alcoholics, drug abusers), the ability to generalize the study findings may be limited.

### Tab: Judgmental Sampling

Judgmental sampling occurs when subjects are chosen for a study based on the professional judgment of the researcher.

Example: The researcher wants to study the characteristics of students who graduate high school before the age of 16. It would not benefit the researcher to study all high school students; therefore, a very specific judgmental sample is chosen.

### Tab: Quota Sampling

Quota sampling is a tailored sample that matches some characteristics of the target population.

Example: If the target population is 30% male and 70% female, the sample should reflect those percentages.

### Image Transcript: Classification of Sampling Methods

Sampling methods—Nonprobability

- convenience sampling
- snowball sampling
- judgmental sampling
- quota sampling

## Section 18: Reducing Sampling Bias in Nonprobability Sampling

The use of nonprobability sampling often introduces bias into the sample because some members of the population are more likely to be included than others. The findings will be an inaccurate representation of the target population. However, there are ways to limit bias, including:

- Clearly define the target population.
- Match the sampling frame as closely as possible to the target population.
- Avoid the researcher's personal involvement in subject selection.
- Increase the sample size.
- Assign subjects to groups randomly once selected.
- Follow up with non-responders.

Example:

Researchers want to study the rates of hypertension among subjects who identify as "straight" and those who identify as "LGBTQ+" (Vaughan, 2017). They do not have the time or money to assess all straight and LGBTQ+ people in the population, so a 100-subject sample from the population is chosen.

When the sample is randomly selected, approximately 96 subjects will identify as straight and about 4 will identify as LGBTQ+. Because this sample underrepresents the LGBTQ+ population, the researchers decide to continue to sample to ensure more LGBTQ+ subjects are surveyed. This sampling process is called oversampling (Vaughan, 2017).

### Reference

Vaughan, R. (2017). Oversampling in health surveys: Why, when, and how? *American Journal of Public Health*, *107*(8), 1214-1215. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2017.303895

## Section 19: Sample Size and Power

Although subject selection determines generalizability, the number of subjects in the sample affects whether the results can be trusted. Sample size influences the ability to detect significant findings and the level of confidence to incorporate those findings into practice and expect a similar outcome.

### Tab: Qualitative Studies

In qualitative studies, sample size is rarely predetermined. The standard for sample size is the achievement of redundancy and saturation (when the researcher concludes that responses are repetitive and no new information is being generated).

The achievement of saturation is determined by the researcher with no formula or calculation used. Documenting saturation is one way that the qualitative researcher can improve the trustworthiness of a study.

### Tab: Quantitative Studies

The standard for determining sample size in quantitative studies is power. Adequate power means that there are enough subjects to detect a difference in the outcome of interest.

Power is calculated using a mathematical process and is often done before sampling to determine the correct number of subjects needed. A power analysis estimates the sample size needed while considering the characteristics of the study and population.

Low power in a study reduces the likelihood that results will be significant. It also affects the reader's confidence in the findings. In general, larger samples are more desirable.

## Section 21: Analyzing the Sampling Section of a Research Study

When reading and evaluating a research study, pay attention to its sampling strategy and sample size. An appropriate sampling strategy allows for generalization of findings and adequate sample size allows for confidence in the results.

Also, pay attention to whether the sample is biased. Remember that bias can be reduced by using objective inclusion and exclusion criteria. When reviewing a qualitative or quantitative study, ask the following questions respectively to evaluate the sampling strategy.

### Image Transcript: Evaluation of Sampling Strategy

**Qualitative Study**

Ask the following questions:

- Were the criteria established for the characteristics that were desirable in informants?
- Did the researchers apply enough effort to find respondents who could best inform the question?
- Was saturation achieved and documented?

**Quantitative Study**

Ask the following questions:

- Were the subjects selected in an objective way?
- Was the sampling strategy applied consistently?
- Were the subjects assigned to treatment groups randomly?
- Were enough subjects included for you to have confidence in the results?

## Section 22: Finding the Sampling Section of a Research Study

A description of the sampling strategy should appear in the "Methods" section of the research article and describe inclusion and exclusion criteria. The description of the sample, including demographics, will likely appear in the "Results" section of the article. Remember that the terms "probability sample" and "random sample" mean the same thing.

Although calculations of power are common, they may not be reported. If the results of the study are statistically significant, this is not a problem, regardless of sample size. If the results are not statistically significant, then reporting a power analysis is necessary and power should be at least 80% or 0.8.

In some articles, the sampling strategy may not be described at all. If the description is unclear or not present, it is safe to assume that the sample was not randomly selected and is most likely a convenience sample. The term "nonprobability sample" may be used.

## Section 23: Let's Review!

Creating an adequate sampling strategy is essential to ensure applicable study findings. Here is a checklist for evaluating the sampling strategy when reading a research article.

### Image Transcript: Sampling Strategy Checklist

- The target population is clearly defined.
  - Description may include clinical, demographic, or behavioral characteristics.
- The inclusion criteria are specific and relevant.
  - Define the main characteristics of the desired population.
- The exclusion criteria are specified.
  - Indicates subjects that are not suitable for the research study.
- Procedures for sampling are described.
  - Quantitative study: Sample is representative of the target population.
  - Qualitative study: Sample can best inform the research question.
- Potential for sampling bias has been identified and controlled.
  - The sample is not affected by homogeneity, nonresponse, or attrition.
- The sample size is adequate and documented by power (quantitative) or saturation (qualitative).
  - Quantitative power or acknowledgement of data saturation is reported.
