## Section 1: Introducation: Nursing Care: Gastroenteritis

# Nursing Care: Gastroenteritis

This lesson provides an overview of how the nurse applies the nursing process in caring for clients with gastroenteritis.

Gastroenteritis, or diarrhea involving the stomach and small intestines, is a common disorder among children. Infants are most susceptible to experiencing dehydration because their body water content is higher than older children and adults.

## Section 3: Explore: Gastroenteritis

# Gastroenteritis

Diarrhea is caused by abnormal intestinal water and electrolyte transport.

### Tab: Types of Diarrhea

- gastroenteritis: involving the stomach and intestines
- enteritis: involving the small intestines
- colitis: involving the colon
- enterocolitis: involving the colon and intestines

### Tab: Classifications of Diarrhea

- acute
  - usually self-limited up to 14 days
  - subsides without specific treatment
  - caused by a variety of viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens
- chronic
  - an increase in stool frequency and increased water content lasting more than 14 days
  - caused by chronic conditions such as malabsorption syndromes, inflammatory bowel disease, immunodeficiency, food allergy, lactose intolerance, or chronic nonspecific diarrhea, or as a result of inadequate management of acute diarrhea

### Tab: Possible Outcomes

- dehydration
  - fluid loss from frequent, watery stools or vomiting
  - reduced fluid intake from nausea or anorexia
  - insensible fluid loss from fever
- electrolyte imbalance
  - loss of sodium, chloride, potassium, and bicarbonate
  - inadequate electrolyte replacement
- metabolic acidosis
  - accumulation of lactic acid
  - loss of bicarbonate
  - malnutrition from inadequate carbohydrate intake or from depleted glycogen stores

Rotavirus is easily spread among infants and young children and can cause severe diarrhea, vomiting, fever, and abdominal pain. The rotavirus vaccine, introduced in 2006, has significantly reduced the incidence of rotavirus (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024). Prior to the introduction of the vaccine, almost all children in the U.S. had rotavirus before the age of 5 years (CDC, 2024).

**Reference:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. (2024). *Clinical overview of rotavirus.* https://www.cdc.gov/rotavirus/hcp/clinical-overview/

## Section 4: Explore: Gastroenteritis: Diagnostic Evaluation

# Gastroenteritis: Diagnostic Evaluation

- Assess clinical manifestations of dehydration status.
  - mild
    - increased thirst
    - slightly dry mucous membranes
  - moderate
    - loss of skin turgor
    - dry mucous membranes
    - sunken eyes
    - sunken fontanel
  - severe
    - signs of moderate dehydration plus 1 of the following
      - rapid thready pulse
      - cyanosis
      - rapid breathing
      - lethargy, coma
- Assess other symptoms such as fever, vomiting, stool characteristics, urinary output, intake.
- Obtain a history to determine possible cause of diarrhea.
  - exposure (daycare attendance)
  - recent treatment with antibiotics
  - diet changes
  - exposure to untreated drinking water
  - contact with animals/birds
  - travel
- Laboratory tests are indicated for severe dehydration and when receiving intravenous fluid therapy.
  - usually elevated in acute diarrhea and should normalize with rehydration
    - hemoglobin
    - hematocrit
    - creatinine
    - blood urea nitrogen (BUN)

## Section 5: Explore: Gastroenteritis: Therapeutic Management

# Gastroenteritis: Therapeutic Management

- Goals
  - assessment of fluid and electrolyte imbalance
  - rehydration
  - maintenance fluid therapy
  - reintroduction of an adequate diet
- Oral rehydration therapy
  - preferred to intravenous fluid therapy because it is effective, safer, less painful, and less costly than intravenous rehydration
  - administer frequent, but small amounts of fluid
- Oral electrolyte solutions
  - enhance and promote the reabsorption of sodium and water
  - reduces vomiting, duration of illness, and the need for intravenous infusions
  - fruit juices, full-strength sports drinks, gelatins, or soft drinks should not be used because they can make diarrhea worse due to the high carbohydrate content
- Intravenous fluid therapy may be needed with severe dehydration, uncontrolled vomiting, inability to drink, or severe gastric distention

> **Nursing alert:** Infants are more prone to becoming dehydrated than older children because of their higher body surface-to-volume ratio and smaller fluid reserves.

### Table: Rehydration Therapy by Degree of Dehydration

| Degree of Dehydration | Rehydration Therapy | Replacement of Losses | Nutrition |
|---|---|---|---|
| minimal or mild | N/A | Infants: give 2-4 ounces of oral rehydration solution for every large watery stool. Children over 1 year: give 4-8 ounces of oral rehydration solution after every large watery stool | Infants: continue breastfeeding or formula and cereal or solids as tolerated. Children over 1 year: continue solid foods as tolerated (starchy foods are easy to digest) |
| mild to moderate | Oral rehydration solution 50-100 mL/kg over 3 to 4 hours. Begin with 1 teaspoon at a time and increase as tolerated | Infants: give 2-4 ounces of oral rehydration solution for every large watery stool. Children over 1 year: give 4-8 ounces of oral rehydration solution after every large watery stool | Infants: continue breastfeeding or formula and cereal or solids as tolerated. Children over 1 year: continue solid foods as tolerated (starchy foods are easy to digest) |
| severe | Intravenous fluid replacement | Infants: give 2-4 ounces of oral rehydration solution for every large watery stool. Children over 1 year: give 4-8 ounces of oral rehydration solution after every large watery stool. If unable to drink, administer intravenous fluids | Infants: continue breastfeeding or formula and cereal or solids as tolerated. Children over 1 year: continue solid foods as tolerated (starchy foods are easy to digest) |

## Section 6: Explore: Gastroenteritis: Nursing Care

# Gastroenteritis: Nursing Care

*(Carousel — 3 slides)*

### Slide 1: Goals

- Maintain adequate hydration.
- Maintain tissue integrity.
- Resume normal elimination.

### Slide 2: Home Care

- Most often, acute diarrhea can be managed at home.
- Caregivers should be educated to monitor for signs of dehydration, the amount of fluid intake, and the frequency and amount of stools.
- Educate about oral rehydration therapy (only indicated if dehydrated) and resuming a normal diet. Administer small quantities of oral rehydration solutions at frequent intervals.
- Vomiting is not a contraindication for oral replacement therapy, unless it is severe.
- Introducing a normal diet may initially result in temporary increase in higher stool output.
- Ensure proper and frequent handwashing and cleaning of surfaces to reduce spread of infection.

### Slide 3: Hospitalization

- Obtain accurate weight; closely monitor intake and output.
- Urine output is essential (indicate adequate renal blood flow) to including potassium in intravenous fluids.
- Monitor peripheral intravenous catheter site and intravenous fluid infusion ensuring correct fluids and rate.
- Collect ordered laboratory specimens (including stool).
- Provide diligent skin care to protect from skin excoriation from diarrhea.
- Ensure proper and frequent handwashing and cleaning of surfaces to reduce spread of infection.
