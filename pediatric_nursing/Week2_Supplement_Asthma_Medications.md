## The core distinction

Every asthma medication is either a **rescue** drug or a **controller** drug. Nearly every exam question reduces to knowing which is which and when each is used.

| | **Rescue / quick-relief** | **Controller / long-term** |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Stop an attack already happening | Prevent attacks from starting |
| **When taken** | PRN, at the first sign of symptoms | **Every day, on schedule** |
| **Onset** | Minutes | Days to weeks for full effect |
| **If symptoms improve** | Use less — that's expected | **Keep taking it** |
| **Main classes** | Short-acting beta<sub>2</sub>-agonist (SABA) | Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) |
| **Examples** | Albuterol, levalbuterol | Fluticasone, budesonide |

## Rescue medications

- **Short-acting beta<sub>2</sub>-agonists (SABA)** — albuterol, levalbuterol. First-line for acute bronchospasm. Onset in minutes. **This is the priority intervention in an acute exacerbation.**
- **Anticholinergics** — ipratropium. Adjunct to SABA in moderate-to-severe exacerbations.
- **Systemic corticosteroids** — oral or IV prednisone/prednisolone. Given for exacerbations to reduce airway inflammation; not a quick-relief drug on their own since onset takes hours.

Side effects of SABAs — tachycardia, tremor, jitteriness, nervousness — are expected and dose-related. Increasing rescue-inhaler use is a red flag that asthma control is worsening and the regimen needs review.

## Controller medications

- **Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)** — fluticasone, budesonide, beclomethasone. **The most effective long-term control medication.** Taken daily.
- **Leukotriene receptor antagonists** — montelukast (Singulair). Oral, often used in young children or as an add-on.
- **Long-acting beta<sub>2</sub>-agonists (LABA)** — salmeterol, formoterol. **Never used alone** — always combined with an ICS. A LABA as monotherapy increases the risk of severe asthma events.
- **Mast cell stabilizers** — cromolyn. Prophylactic; can be used before exercise or a known exposure.
- **Biologics** — omalizumab and similar, reserved for severe allergic asthma.

## Teaching points the quiz targets

- **Do not stop the controller when the child feels better.** This is the single most-tested misconception. Symptom improvement means the controller is *working*; stopping it lets airway inflammation return. Only the prescriber changes the regimen.
- **Rinse the mouth after every ICS dose** to prevent oral candidiasis (thrush) and hoarseness.
- **Use a spacer** with a metered-dose inhaler — improves lung deposition and reduces oropharyngeal deposition. Young children add a mask.
- **The rescue inhaler goes everywhere with the child**, including school.
- **Annual influenza vaccine** is recommended — respiratory infection is a major trigger.
- **Eliminate secondhand smoke exposure.**
- A **short-acting** bronchodilator, not a long-acting one, is the answer to "acute exacerbation, what first?"

## Why the missed question was what it was

> *"We can stop the controller medication once our child's symptoms improve."* — This is the statement that **indicates a need for further teaching.**

The other three options are all correct understanding: using the rescue inhaler PRN for difficulty breathing, getting the annual flu vaccine, and limiting secondhand smoke. Controller medications are daily maintenance therapy that suppress the underlying chronic airway inflammation. The absence of symptoms is evidence the drug is working, not evidence it is no longer needed.

**Pattern to carry into the exam:** on "which statement indicates a need for further teaching," you are hunting for the *wrong* statement. Stopping a maintenance medication because the patient feels better is almost always that wrong statement — it recurs across asthma, seizures, hypertension, and antibiotics.
