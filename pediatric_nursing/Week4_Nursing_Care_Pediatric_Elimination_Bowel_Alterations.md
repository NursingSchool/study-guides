## Section 1: Introduction: Bowel Dysfunction

# Care of the Child with Gastrointestinal Dysfunction

This lesson provides an overview of pediatric gastrointestinal (GI) disorders and how the nurse applies the nursing process in caring for pediatric clients with GI disorders. The primary function of the GI tract is the digestion and absorption of nutrients. Thus, any dysfunction of the GI tract can cause significant problems with the exchange of fluids, electrolytes, and nutrients.

This lesson will focus on the nursing care of children with constipation, Hirschsprung disease, gastroesophageal reflux, appendicitis, pyloric stenosis, and intussusception.

## Section 3: Explore: Gastrointestinal Dysfunction

# Gastrointestinal Dysfunction

The most common consequences of gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction in children include malabsorption, fluid and electrolyte disturbances, malnutrition, and poor growth. The most important basic nursing assessments include measurement of intake and output, height and weight, abdominal examination, and simple stool and urine tests.

There are several types of GI dysfunction in children which can be classified as general GI disorders, motility disorders, inflammatory conditions, obstructive disorders, malabsorption syndromes, and structural defects. Below are examples of each classification of GI dysfunction.

This lesson will focus on constipation, Hirschsprung disease, gastroesophageal reflux, appendicitis, pyloric stenosis, and intussusception.

### Image Hotspots: Classifications of GI Dysfunction

**Motility Disorders**

**Inflammatory Conditions**

- appendicitis

**Obstructive Disorders**

- paralytic ileus

**Malabsorption Syndromes**

- abdominal wall defects (omphalocele, gastroschisis)
- hernia

## Section 4: Explore: Gastrointestinal Function Assessment

# Gastrointestinal Function Assessment

Common abnormal gastrointestinal assessment findings:

- abdominal distention
- hypoactive, hyperactive, or absent bowel sounds
- nausea/vomiting
- spitting up/regurgitation
- projectile vomiting
  - typically associated with pyloric stenosis
- diarrhea/constipation
- fever
  - common manifestation of illness in children
  - usually associated with dehydration, infection, or inflammation
- failure to thrive
  - failure to maintain established growth pattern or consistently remaining below the 5th percentile for height and weight on standard growth charts
  - sometimes accompanied by developmental delays

## Section 5: Explore: Nursing Care for Gastrointestinal Surgery

# Nursing Care for Gastrointestinal Surgery

### Accordion: Gastrointestinal Surgery Types

- Open Surgery: A large incision is made in the abdomen to complete the surgery.
- Laparoscopic Surgery: Small incisions are made in the abdomen and a small camera, light, and special tools are used to do surgery through the small cuts. Recovery is usually much shorter.

### Accordion: Preoperative Nursing Care

- Consult child life specialist to help the child and families cope with the hospital experience.

Watch this video by Maimonides Medical Center (2012) to learn more about the role of a child life specialist. *(Video — no in-page transcript available. [NEEDS MANUAL REVIEW])*

- Maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) status.
- Insert peripheral intravenous catheter and administer prescribed intravenous fluids to maintain fluid balance.
- If indicated, insert nasogastric tube with intermittent suction to remove stomach contents and decompress the stomach.
- Administer preoperative antibiotics as prescribed.
- Prepare surgical consent and witness informed consent.

### Accordion: Postoperative Care

- Monitor vital signs per postoperative protocol.
- Monitor dressing/wound for redness or drainage.
- Assess for return of bowel sounds.
- Assess pain using appropriate pain scale and administer prescribed pain medication.
- Assess intake and output.
- Administer postoperative antibiotics as prescribed.
- Administer intravenous fluids (decrease intravenous fluids as oral (PO) intake increases).
- Slowly introduce PO intake as ordered and tolerated.
- Monitor and care for drains.

## Section 6: Explore: Gastrointestinal Disorder: Constipation

# Gastrointestinal Disorder: Constipation

Constipation is an alteration in the frequency, consistency, or ease of passing stool. The frequency of bowel movements varies by age, but children 4 years and older can be diagnosed with constipation if they have fewer than three stools per week. Constipation is often associated with painful bowel movements, blood-streaked or retained stool, abdominal pain, lack of appetite, and stool incontinence.

### Tab: Causes of Constipation

- structural disorders
  - strictures
  - ectopic anus
  - Hirschsprung disease (HD)
- systemic disorders
  - hypothyroidism
  - hypercalcemia
  - chronic lead poisoning
- medications
  - antacids
  - diuretics
  - antiepileptics
  - antihistamines
  - opioids
  - iron supplementation
- spinal cord lesions
  - related to loss of rectal tone and sensation
- idiopathic
  - no underlying cause can be identified
  - most common type of constipation
- environmental or psychosocial factors
  - transient illness
  - withholding and avoidance secondary to painful or negative experiences with stooling
  - dietary intake with decreased fluid and fiber

### Tab: Age Specific Considerations

- newborns
  - Should pass a first meconium stool within 24 to 36 hours of birth.
  - If not, newborn should be assessed for intestinal atresia/stenosis, Hirschsprung disease, hypothyroidism, meconium plug, or meconium ileus.
- infants
  - Breastfed infants have softer stool than bottle-fed infants, so constipation is less common.
  - Breastfed infants may have fewer stools because breastmilk is digested with little residue.
  - Constipation is more common when infants are switched to cow's milk, so adding vegetables or fruit to the diet often corrects the problem.
- children
  - Most often attributed to normal development when a child has control over bodily functions (potty training).
  - A child who has had pain during bowel movements may withhold stool, causing further accumulation of stool, causing more pain during bowel movements.
  - School-age children may experience constipation if they do not want to use the school bathrooms or are hurried in the morning leaving for school.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- treatment depends on the cause; may involve diet, medication, enemas, or surgery.
- management of simple constipation
  - goal: promote regular bowel movements
  - diet changes
  - stool softeners: docusate or lactulose
  - laxatives: polyethylene glycol (Miralax) which can be mixed in any beverage
- management of chronic constipation
  - goals: restore regular evacuation of stool, shrink distended rectum to normal size, and promote a regular toileting routine
  - takes months or years to resolve
  - treatment: bowel cleansing, maintenance therapy to prevent stool retention, modification of diet, bowel habit training, and behavioral modification

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- Assess bowel habits and medications.
- Educate about normal stool patterns, dietary changes, medications, and enemas.

### Image Transcript: Bristol Stool Chart

- Type 1: Separate hard lumps (Severe constipation)
- Type 2: Lumpy and sausage-like (Mild constipation)
- Type 3: Sausage shape with cracks (Normal)
- Type 4: Like a smooth soft sausage or snake (Normal)
- Type 5: Soft blobs with clear-cut edges (Lacking fiber)
- Type 6: Mushy consistency with ragged edges (Mild diarrhea)
- Type 7: Liquid consistency with no solid pieces (Severe diarrhea)

## Section 8: Explore: Motility Disorder: Hirschsprung Disease

# Motility Disorder: Hirschsprung Disease

Hirschsprung disease (HD) is a congenital anomaly that results in mechanical obstruction from inadequate motility of part of the intestine. It accounts for about one-fourth of all cases of neonatal intestinal obstruction. It is four times more common in males than in females.

**Image (alt text):** Normal large intestine on left, right shows Hirschsprungs disease with enlarged colon, collapsed rectum, and lack of nerves

### Tab: Pathophysiology

The pathology of HD relates to the absence of ganglion cells in the affected areas of the intestine, resulting in a loss of the rectosphincteric reflex and an abnormal microenvironment of the cells of the affected intestine.

### Tab: Age Specific Clinical Manifestations

- most diagnosed in the first few months of life
- newborns
  - distended abdomen
  - feeding intolerance with bilious vomiting
  - refusal to feed
  - delay in the passage of meconium
- infants
  - failure to thrive
  - constipation
  - abdominal distention
  - episodes of diarrhea and vomiting
  - signs of enterocolitis
- children
  - constipation
  - ribbonlike, foul-smelling stools
  - abdominal distention
  - visible peristalsis
  - easily palpable fecal mass
  - undernourished, anemic appearance

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- Contrast enema will show an area of the narrow aganglionic distal segment and the dilated proximal colon (megacolon), and this may not be evident until 2 months of age or later.
- Rectal biopsy is done to confirm diagnosis.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- Most require surgical removal of aganglionic portion of the bowel.
- A temporary colostomy may be needed.

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- newborn
  - Help parents adjust to the congenital defect.
  - Foster bonding.
  - Prepare for surgical intervention.
- preoperative
  - Administer enemas for bowel emptying,
  - Measure abdominal circumference and monitor abdominal distention.
  - Maintain nothing oral (NPO) status.
  - Give intravenous fluids.
  - Prepare surgical consent.
- postoperative
  - Involve parents in care.
  - Promote return to feedings.
  - Some children may require daily anal dilation at home to avoid stricture.
  - Care for colostomy if needed.

## Section 9: Explore: Motility Disorder: Gastroesophageal Reflux

# Motility Disorder: Gastroesophageal Reflux

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is when gastric contents from the stomach move up into the esophagus. This can occur at any time, but most often is after meals and at night. Most infants who have GER symptoms grow out of it by 1 year of age. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is the result of GER complications such as failure to thrive, respiratory problems, or dysphagia.

**Image (alt text):** Nissen Fundoplication, left showing fundus wrapped around lower esophageal sphincter, right showing the sutures below diaphragm which secure it.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

The primary cause is inappropriate relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter, which allows acidic stomach contents to rise into the esophagus.

### Tab: Age Specific Clinical Manifestations

- infants
  - spitting up
  - regurgitation
  - recurrent vomiting (may be forceful)
  - excessive crying
  - irritability
  - arching of the back
  - stiffening
  - poor weight gain
  - respiratory problems (e.g., cough, wheeze, stridor, gagging, choking with feedings)
  - feeding refusal
- children
  - heartburn
  - abdominal pain
  - chronic cough
  - hoarse voice
  - dysphagia
  - asthma
  - recurrent vomiting

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- GER can usually be diagnosed by a history and physical examination.
- An upper gastrointestinal series can be used to evaluate anatomic abnormalities.
- A 24-hour intraesophageal pH monitoring study Is not very reliable in the diagnosis of GER in infants and children.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- Therapeutic management of GER depends on its severity.
- Avoid foods that exacerbate acid reflux.
- Eat small, frequent meals.
  - infants- thickened feeds (1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon of rice cereal per ounce of formula) and position upright after feedings
- Give medications.
  - H2-receptor antagonists such as ranitidine (Zantac) or proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole (Prilosec)
- Surgical management is Nissen fundoplication.
  - Reserved for those who fail other therapy or have severe or life-threatening complications such as recurrent aspiration pneumonia, apnea, severe esophagitis, or failure to thrive.

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- Identify children with symptoms suggestive of GER.
- Educate parents regarding home care, including feeding, positioning, and medications.
- If a Nissen fundoplication is performed, postoperative care includes nasogastric (NG) tube or gastrostomy tube for gastric decompression (notify surgeon if the NG tube is accidentally removed, do not replace).
  - Slowly begin feedings and vent tubing after feedings initially.
  - Educate caregivers for home care.

## Section 11: Explore: Inflammatory Condition: Appendicitis

# Inflammatory Condition: Appendicitis

Appendicitis, inflammation of the vermiform appendix (blind sac at the end of the cecum), is the most common cause of emergency abdominal surgery in childhood.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

When the appendix becomes blocked, it can become inflamed and swollen and can rupture. A ruptured appendix is a medical emergency and can result in an abscess and/or peritonitis.

### Tab: Clinical Manifestations

- Classically, the first symptom of appendicitis is periumbilical pain, followed by nausea, right lower quadrant pain at McBurney's point, and, later, vomiting with fever.
- Lethargy, anorexia, constipation, irritability, diarrhea, stooped posture, decreased or absent bowel sounds are all additional clinical manifestations of appendicitis.
- Perforation of the appendix can occur within approximately 48 hours of the initial complaint of pain. A sign of rupture is a sudden relief from pain.
- Signs of peritonitis include subsequent increase in pain, abdominal guarding, abdominal distention, tachypnea, and chills.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- Diagnosis is based primarily on the history and physical examination. Palpating the abdomen should be delayed until all other assessments have been made.
- Laboratory studies usually include a complete blood count (CBC); urinalysis (to rule out a urinary tract infection); and, in adolescent females, serum human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG; to rule out an ectopic pregnancy), and C-reactive protein (CRP).
- Ultrasound or computed tomography (CT) scan should also be completed.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- Treatment of appendicitis that has not ruptured includes intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and an appendectomy (surgical removal).
- Treatment of a ruptured appendix includes intravenous fluids, antibiotics, NG suction, and peritoneal cavity irrigation.

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- Preparation for surgery includes maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) status, intravenous fluids, antibiotics, education, and preparing surgical consent.
- Postoperative care includes peripheral intravenous line, antibiotics, pain management, nasogastric tube to suction, dressing changes, and care of drain (if placed).

### Image Transcript: Appendicitis

Appendicitis is the inflammation of the appendix, a thin, tube-like organ attached to the large intestine. During appendicitis, the appendix becomes enlarged and may rupture. Although people of all ages can develop appendicitis, it most frequently occurs in patients between ages 10 and 30.

Appendicitis occurs when the appendix becomes blocked by bacterial infection, stagnation of waste matter, parasites, inflammatory bowel disease, or other gastrointestinal conditions.

## Section 13: Explore: Obstructive Disorder: Pyloric Stenosis

# Obstructive Disorder: Pyloric Stenosis

Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis occurs when the pyloric sphincter muscle becomes a thickened outlet obstruction. As a result, this causes compensatory dilation, hypertrophy, and hyperperistalsis of the stomach. The condition usually develops in the first few weeks of life and is more common in males.

**Image (alt text):** Stomach showing thickened muscle at pylorus, leading to a narrow passage between stomach and duodenum.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

The pyloric canal between the stomach and duodenum narrows and becomes inflamed and edematous.

### Tab: Clinical Manifestations

- Initially, infants will have nonbilious vomiting that becomes projectile vomiting as the obstruction progresses.
- Prolonged vomiting leads to dehydration, weight loss, and failure to thrive.
- Pylorus may be palpable as an olive-shaped mass in the epigastrium just to the right of the umbilicus.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- history and physical exam
- ultrasound

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- pyloromyotomy surgery (usually laparoscopically)

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- observation and recognition of clinical features to help establish the diagnosis
- preoperatively
  - restore hydration and electrolyte balance with intravenous fluids
  - stomach decompression (management of nasogastric tube)
  - prepare surgical consent
- postoperatively
  - intravenous fluids administered until infant is taking and retaining formula by mouth
  - feedings usually restarted with clear liquids in small amounts within 12-24 hours of surgery

## Section 15: Explore: Obstructive Disorder: Intussusception

# Obstructive Disorder: Intussusception

Intussusception is a common cause of intestinal obstruction in children. It is more common in males than females and most often occurs in children under 2 years of age.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

Intussusception occurs when a proximal segment of the bowel telescopes into a more distal segment, pulling the mesentery with it. Compression, pressure, and obstruction can result in ischemia.

### Tab: Clinical Manifestations

- Intussusception usually presents with the sudden onset of crampy abdominal pain, inconsolable crying, and a drawing up of the knees to the chest in an otherwise healthy child.
- Between painful cramping episodes, the child appears normal.
- Other clinical manifestations include vomiting; lethargy; passage of red, currant jelly-like stools (stool mixed with blood and mucus); tender, distended abdomen; palpable sausage-shaped mass in upper right quadrant; empty lower right quadrant (Dance sign); eventual fever, and other signs of peritonitis. If not resolved, this can result in bowel necrosis and perforation.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- Subjective history often leads to a diagnosis.
- Ultrasound provides a definitive diagnosis; ultrasound findings include a heterogenous mass and a "bull's-eye".

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- reduction by gas enema or hydrostatic enema
- surgery if reduction is unsuccessful or for clients who are unstable

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- recognition of clinical features to help establish the diagnosis
- preparation of family for the need for hospitalization, reduction, and possible surgery
- preoperative (if needed): maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) status, intravenous fluids, antibiotics, routine laboratory testing, prepare surgical consent, monitor stools, bowel decompression if bowel is perforated
- postoperative: monitor vital signs, care for sutures and dressing, and assess for the return of bowel sounds

> **Nursing alert:** Passage of a normal brown stool typically means the intussusception has resolved and should be immediately reported to the health care provider.

### Image Transcript: Intussusception

Intussusception is the sliding of one part of the intestine into another. Intussusception is caused by part of the intestine being pulled inward into itself. This can block the passage of food through the intestine. If the blood supply is cut off, the segment of intestine pulled inside can die. The pressure created by the walls of the intestine pressing together causes: decreased blood flow, irritation, and swelling. The intestine can die, and the patient can have significant bleeding. If a hole occurs, infection, shock, and dehydration can take place very rapidly. The cause of intussusception is not known, although viral infections may be responsible in some cases. Sometimes a lymph node, polyp, or tumor can trigger the problem. The older the child, the more likely such a trigger will be found. Intussusception can affect both children and adults, although most cases occur in children ages 6 months - 2 years. It affects boys four times as often as girls. (source: NIH Medline Plus)
