## Section 1: Introduction: Nursing Care: Pediatric Elimination: Urinary Alterations

# Care of the Child with Urinary Dysfunction

This lesson provides an overview of pediatric urinary dysfunction and how the nurse applies the nursing process in caring for pediatric clients with urinary dysfunction. Urinary elimination involves the process of waste formation through the production of urine. The kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra must function for urination to occur. The type and clinical manifestations of urinary dysfunction change with the age of the child. This lesson will focus on the nursing care of children with urinary tract infections, enuresis, and acute poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis.

## Section 3: Explore: Urinary Function Assessment

# Urinary Function Assessment

Many clinical manifestations of kidney or urinary tract dysfunction are common to a number of childhood disorders, so it is important to gather further information from the child's history, family history, and laboratory studies, in addition to the physical examination. The most important basic nursing assessments include accurate measurement of intake and output, height and weight, and blood pressure.

## Age Specific Clinical Manifestations

### Tab: Neonate (birth to 1 month)

- poor feeding
- vomiting
- failure to gain weight
- respiratory distress
- dehydration
- jaundice

### Tab: Infant (1-24 months)

- poor feeding
- vomiting
- failure to gain weight
- excessive thirst
- frequent urination
- fever
- dehydration

### Tab: Child (2-14 years)

- poor appetite
- vomiting
- growth failure
- excessive thirst
- enuresis
- frequent urination
- swelling of the face
- blood in urine
- abdominal or back pain
- hypertension

## Common Laboratory and Radiologic Tests

- urine culture and sensitivity
- glomerular filtration rate
  - generally accepted as best overall indication of kidney function
- creatinine
- blood urea nitrogen (BUN)
- ultrasound
- abdominal x-ray
- voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG)

## Section 5: Explore: Urinary Tract Infection

# Urinary Tract Infection

### Tab: Pathophysiology

- A urinary tract infection (UTI) can occur along any part of the urinary tract, resulting in pyelonephritis, cystitis, and urethritis.
- Escherichia coli is the most common cause of a UTI.
- Urologic abnormalities, genetic factors, and functional abnormalities such as bowel bladder dysfunction can increase the risk of developing a UTI.
- The single most important host factor influencing the occurrence of UTIs is urinary stasis.

### Tab: Age Specific Clinical Manifestations

- newborns
  - fever
  - hypothermia
  - jaundice
  - tachypnea
  - cyanosis
  - appear quite ill
- infants and toddlers under 2 years
  - nonspecific symptoms
  - fever
  - irritability
  - lethargy
  - poor feeding
  - vomiting
  - diarrhea
- children over 2 years
  - classic symptoms of urinary tract infection including enuresis or daytime incontinence in the child who has been toilet trained
  - fever
  - foul-smelling urine
  - increased frequency of urination
  - dysuria
  - urgency
- older children and adolescents
  - lower tract infection
    - frequency and painful urination
    - grossly bloody urine
    - absent or low-grade fever
  - upper tract infection
    - fever
    - chills
    - flank pain
    - often in addition to lower tract symptoms

> **Remember This:** High fever and chills accompanied by flank pain, severe abdominal pain, and leukocytosis suggest pyelonephritis.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- history and physical examination
- urinalysis and culture
  - urine may appear cloudy, hazy, or thick, with noticeable strands of mucus and pus
  - urine may have an unpleasant odor, even when fresh
- contamination of a urine specimen is the most common cause of false-positive results

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- goals
  - eliminate the acute infection
  - prevent complications
  - reduce the likelihood of renal damage
- antibiotic therapy
  - depends on laboratory culture (to identify the organism)
  - depends on sensitivity tests (to identify which antibiotics the organism is sensitive or resistant to)
- common antibiotics to treat urinary tract infections
  - penicillins
  - sulfonamides
  - cephalosporins
- surgical correction of anatomical defects or prophylactic antibiotics may be needed for recurrent infections

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- Identify children with symptoms suggestive of urinary tract infection.
- Collect urine specimens according to age and toilet training status, prior to administering antibiotics.
  - urine bag
  - clean catch
  - bladder catheterization
- Educate parents and children about treatment plan.
- Educate parents and children about prevention of future infections.
  - increase fluid intake
  - females wipe from front to back
  - wear cotton underwear
  - avoid "holding" urine
  - take showers instead of baths

### Image Transcript: Urine Specimen Collection

**Urine Bag:**

- For infants and toddlers who are not toilet trained.
- Clean and dry genitalia, perineum, and surrounding skin.
- Apply bag firmly around genital area.
- Check bag frequently.

**Clean Catch:**

- Clean area. For males, clean the tip of the penis. For females, the perineum is wiped from front to back.
- Void a few milliliters of urine.
- Collect mid-stream urine in sterile specimen cup.

**Bladder Catheterization:**

- Avoid unless necessary.
  - Note: If a urinalysis obtained by a bag specimen is negative, the result could be sufficient; however, if it is positive, a specimen needs to be obtained by bladder catheterization (or suprapubic aspiration by an advanced provider) as recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (Hockenberry et al., 2023).
- Sterile procedure: clean, insert catheter, allow urine to flow into specimen cup

## Section 8: Explore: Enuresis

# Enuresis

Enuresis (bed-wetting) is a common disorder that is defined as intentional or involuntary passage of urine in children who are beyond the age when they should have voluntary bladder control.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

- There is no clear etiology for enuresis, but there are several theories.
  - Children sleep more soundly than others.
  - Insufficient antidiuretic hormone causes an inability to concentrate urine.
  - An unstable bladder detrusor muscle spontaneously contracts to cause bed-wetting.

### Tab: Clinical Manifestations

- Medical evaluation is recommended for those with chronological or developmental age of at least 5 years when enuresis occurs at least twice a week for a minimum of 3 consecutive months.
- Enuresis can cause serious psychological problems in affected children because of impacts on their social life such as not being able to have sleepovers or attend overnight camps.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- A routine physical examination is performed to rule out physical causes such as:
  - urinary tract infection
  - structural disorders of the urinary tract
  - neurologic deficits
  - disorders that increase the normal output of urine
  - disorders that impair the concentrating ability of the kidneys
- Psychiatric evaluation is warranted for psychological difficulties or suspicion of a personality disorder.
- Functional bladder capacity:
  - Have the child hold urine until the strongest urgency is felt, then measure the urine voided.
  - Normal bladder capacity (in ounces) is the child's age plus 2 (up to 14 years of age).
  - A bladder volume of 10 to 12 ounces (300 to 350 ml) is sufficient for retention of a night's urine.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- Enuresis has been treated in several ways:
  - purposeful interruption of sleep to void
  - reward system
  - restriction or elimination of fluids after the evening meal
  - avoidance of caffeinated and sugar-containing beverages after 4 p.m.
  - medications

> **Nursing alert:** It is imperative that punishment not be used to correct enuresis.

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- Provide education, support, and encouragement for child and parent with decided treatment plan.

## Section 9: Explore: Acute Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis

# Acute Poststreptococcal Glomerulonephritis

Acute poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN) is the most common noninfectious renal disease in childhood, primarily affecting early school-age children.

### Tab: Pathophysiology

- APSGN is an immune-complex disease that can occur after infection by certain strains of streptococcus (the antecedent infection).
- The glomerular capillary loops are compromised, causing an excessive accumulation of sodium and water.

### Tab: Clinical Manifestations

- Typically, the child may be well until they experience the antecedent infection. In some cases, there may be no history of an antecedent infection or it may be described as only a cold.
- Signs of APSGN:
  - periorbital, gonadal, abdominal, or lower extremity edema
  - loss of appetite
  - decreased urinary output
  - cola- or tea-colored urine
  - antecedent streptococcal infection
- Major complications of APSGN are hypertensive encephalopathy, acute cardiac decompensation, and acute kidney injury.

### Tab: Diagnostic Evaluation

- Urinalysis may show hematuria, proteinuria, and increased specific gravity.
- Urine cultures are negative.
- Antibody responses to the extracellular products of the streptococci provide indirect evidence of previous streptococcal infection.

### Tab: Therapeutic Management

- Recovery is spontaneous and uneventful in most cases.
- Management consists of general supportive measures and early recognition and treatment of complications.
- Those with substantial edema, hypertension, gross hematuria, or significant oliguria are often hospitalized because of the unpredictability of complications.
- Signs of improvement include an increase in urine output and corresponding decrease in weight as the edema resolves.

### Tab: Nursing Care Management

- identification and treatment of hypertension
- dietary restrictions based on clinical manifestations
  - restrict sodium intake with hypertension/edema
  - restrict potassium intake with oliguria
  - restrict protein from prolonged oliguria
- antibiotics if there is evidence of persistent streptococcal infections

**APSGN**

- edema
- loss of appetite
- history of streptococcal infection
- cola or tea-colored urine
- decreased urine output
